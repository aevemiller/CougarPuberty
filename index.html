Perimenopause and Menopause: What the Science Shows and What Helps

Hot flashes, broken sleep, brain fog and the quieter changes in bone and heart health all trace back to the same hormonal shift. Treatment has changed a great deal in the past two years. This guide gathers what the major medical societies now recommend and explains the biology behind it, with every claim linked to its source at the bottom of the page.

This page is for learning, not diagnosis. It summarizes guidance for healthy women in midlife and can't weigh your own health history, so use it to prepare for a conversation with a clinician who knows you.

Most menopause symptoms are treatable, and the years around the final period matter most for long-term health

Six findings from current guidelines that many women never hear from a doctor.

Perimenopause can start in your early to mid 40s

Cycle changes usually begin six to eight years before the last period.22 New sleep trouble, mood shifts and heavier or irregular periods are often the first clues, sometimes well before hot flashes.

How the stages work

Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes

For healthy women under 60, or within 10 years of menopause, the leading U.S. and European guidelines say the benefits outweigh the risks.1,2,4 Since late 2025 the FDA has been removing the boxed warnings about heart disease, breast cancer and dementia from these products.6

Hormone therapy in detail

There are good options if you can't or don't want to take hormones

Two newer prescription pills block the brain pathway that sets off hot flashes. Certain antidepressants, gabapentin, cognitive behavioral therapy and clinical hypnosis also have solid evidence.1,3

Options without hormones

Vaginal and bladder symptoms tend to get worse without treatment

Low-dose vaginal estrogen treats them well and lowers the risk of repeat urinary tract infections, with very little reaching the rest of the body.5

About 70% of the bone a woman loses from her spine over that decade goes in this short window.27 Heavy strength training with impact helps, and hormone therapy prevents the loss.

Black cohosh, soy isoflavones and other herbal products have not held up in good trials, and they aren't regulated for purity. Guidelines advise against relying on them.1,3

Part 1 · The science

What changes in your hormones, brain and body, and when. Knowing the timing explains both the symptoms and why some prevention steps matter more in certain years.

Perimenopause begins years before the final period and moves through recognizable stages

Menopause itself is a single point in time: the day you reach 12 months without a period. Perimenopause is the run-up to it, and it's where most of the symptoms start. Researchers divide the transition into stages based on what your cycle is doing, because cycle changes tell you where you are more reliably than a blood test can.22

Late 30s to 40s

Late reproductive years

Cycles are still regular but may get shorter or change in flow. Fertility is already falling.

About 6 to 8 years before

Early transition

Your cycle length starts to vary by a week or more from what's normal for you.

About 2 years before

Late transition

You go 60 days or more without a period at least once. Hot flashes become common.

Usually 45 to 55

Menopause

Twelve months in a row without a period. The final period is only recognized in hindsight.

First 5 to 8 years after

Early postmenopause

Hormones settle at their new, lower levels. Hot flashes often continue for years.

After that

Later postmenopause

Symptoms usually ease, and attention turns to long-term health.

You can still get pregnant during perimenopause. If you use a non-hormonal method, guidelines suggest continuing birth control until 12 months without a period if you're 50 or older, or 24 months if you're younger. Hormone therapy doses don't prevent pregnancy.39,43

Estrogen swings unpredictably during perimenopause while progesterone falls

The usual picture of menopause is a slow, steady fade in estrogen. Perimenopause is messier than that. As the supply of eggs dwindles, the brain pushes harder to get the ovaries to respond, and some cycles end up with unusually high estrogen while others produce very little. Progesterone, which the ovary makes after releasing an egg, drops earlier and more steadily because ovulation becomes less reliable.23 After the final period, progesterone stops and estrogen settles at a low, stable level.

Those swings explain why perimenopause can feel worse than menopause for some women. Heavy or unpredictable bleeding, breast tenderness and premenstrual-style mood changes can intensify before things calm down.

This is also why a single blood test usually isn't useful. On any given day your levels can look normal, high or low. European endocrinology guidelines say hormone testing isn't needed to diagnose perimenopause or menopause in women over 45; your cycle pattern and symptoms tell the story.4 Testing can make sense for women under 45, or under 40, when other causes need to be ruled out.

Hot flashes start in the brain's temperature center and often last seven years or more

A hot flash is a misfire in the hypothalamus, the small region at the base of the brain that keeps your body temperature in range. Estrogen normally keeps a group of nerve cells there in check. When estrogen falls, those cells grow larger and more active and send a chemical messenger called neurokinin B to the brain's thermostat.24 The thermostat reads this as overheating and dumps heat: blood vessels in the skin open, you flush and sweat, and your heart may race. Then you feel chilled as your body overcorrects.

Hot flashes last far longer than the six months to two years that older guidance suggested. In a large U.S. study that followed women through the transition for up to 17 years, half of those with frequent hot flashes had them for more than seven years, and they typically kept going about four and a half years after the final period. Women whose hot flashes began early in perimenopause had them longest, often for more than a decade. Black women had them longer than any other group studied.20

Up to 80% of women get hot flashes at some point. Frequent ones are linked to poorer sleep and mood and to less favorable blood pressure and cholesterol, which is one more reason to treat them and to get your heart health checked.17,20

The brain adapts to falling estrogen, and memory and mood can dip during the transition

Estrogen acts throughout the brain, including the areas that handle memory, attention, mood, sleep and temperature. Brain imaging published in 2024 found that as women move through the transition, their brains carry more estrogen receptors, which the researchers read as the brain working to capture what estrogen remains. Women with higher receptor levels in memory regions tended to score lower on memory tests.25

Brain fog is real, and for most women it's temporary. In a long-term study of more than 2,000 midlife women, the normal improvement people show when they repeat a memory or speed test stalled during perimenopause, then returned to its usual pace after menopause.26

Perimenopause is also a recognized window of higher risk for depression, including for women who have never been depressed before. Women with a past history of depression have a higher chance of it returning in these years, and night sweats that break up sleep make everything harder.14 Low mood that lasts more than a couple of weeks or gets in the way of daily life deserves treatment in its own right, and antidepressants and talk therapy remain the first-line treatments.1,14

If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the U.S. to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time of day.

Many of the body changes linked to menopause cluster in the few years around the final period

Estrogen helps maintain bone, muscle, blood vessels, skin and the tissues of the vagina and bladder. When it falls, change in those tissues speeds up on a fairly predictable schedule. Knowing the schedule tells you when prevention pays off most.

Bone

Bone loss speeds up about a year before the final period and is fastest for the two years after. Women who lose bone faster in this window go on to have more fractures, regardless of how dense their bones were to begin with.27,29

Muscle and joints

Muscle mass declines and body fat rises during the transition. More than 70% of women report joint or muscle pain at some point, and about a quarter find it disabling. Frozen shoulder is also more common in these years. Doctors have started calling this cluster the musculoskeletal syndrome of menopause.17,30

Heart and metabolism

LDL cholesterol rises during the transition faster than aging alone would explain, and fat shifts toward the belly and around the organs. Insulin resistance climbs fastest in the same few years. Heart disease is the leading cause of death in women, and this is when the risk starts to climb.17,28

Skin

Skin can lose up to 30% of its collagen in the first five years after menopause, then about 2% a year. It becomes thinner and drier, and wounds heal more slowly. In studies, estrogen therapy slowed this loss.31

Vagina and bladder

Thinning tissue can cause dryness, painful sex, burning, urgency and repeat urinary tract infections. Doctors call this genitourinary syndrome of menopause. Unlike hot flashes, it tends to persist or worsen without treatment.5

Biological aging

Studies that estimate biological age from chemical marks on DNA find that an earlier menopause goes along with faster aging of blood cells, and chronic insomnia does too. Women who had used hormone therapy looked biologically younger in one tissue. These are observational findings, which can't prove cause and effect.32

Guidelines from professional medical societies are the most reliable source of menopause advice

Menopause care has become a crowded market. Social media is full of confident claims, and some clinics sell treatments with little evidence behind them. The most dependable answers come from guidelines written by expert panels that grade the strength of the evidence and disclose conflicts of interest. These are the ones this page relies on.

For most healthy women under 60, hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes

Menopausal hormone therapy replaces some of the estrogen your ovaries stop making. If you still have a uterus, you also take a progestogen (progesterone or a similar hormone), because estrogen alone can overstimulate the uterine lining and raise the risk of uterine cancer. If you've had a hysterectomy, you take estrogen alone.2,4

Nothing else works as well for hot flashes, night sweats and the sleep they disrupt. ACOG gives this a strong recommendation, with the advice to start before age 60 or within 10 years of menopause.1 Hormone therapy also prevents bone loss and reduces fractures.2,4 One alternative pairs estrogen with bazedoxifene, which protects the uterine lining in place of a progestogen; ACOG suggests it as an option.1

The fear of hormone therapy traces back to a 2002 study of mostly older women

In 2002, a large federally funded trial stopped its estrogen-plus-progestin arm early because women taking the pills had more breast cancer, heart attacks, strokes and blood clots. Prescriptions collapsed, from about 22% of women to about 4%, and the FDA put its most serious boxed warning on every estrogen product.42

Later analyses told a story that depended on age. Nearly two-thirds of the women in that trial were 60 or older, many years past menopause.1 A 2025 reanalysis found that for women in their 50s with hot flashes, hormone therapy relieved symptoms without raising heart disease risk, while women who started in their 70s had a clear increase. The picture for women in their 60s remains uncertain.21

U.S. regulators began removing most of the boxed warning from hormone therapy in late 2025

  1. November 10, 2025

    The FDA announced it would remove the boxed warning language on heart disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from hormone therapy products.6,7

  2. February 12, 2026

    The first six products received updated labels, including an estradiol gel, a progesterone capsule and a vaginal estrogen ring.6

  3. October 15, 2026

    ACOG published its new guideline on hot flashes, sleep and mood, which notes the label changes and supports starting therapy before 60 or within 10 years of menopause.1

  4. October 30, 2026

    Three estradiol patches received updated labels. Twenty-nine companies have submitted changes, so more labels will follow.6

One warning stays in place: women with a uterus need a progestogen along with estrogen to protect against uterine cancer.7 The FDA has also said it is exploring making vaginal estrogen available without a prescription.6

The change has supporters and critics. Many gynecologists say the old warning scared away women who stood to benefit. Critics, including some researchers who led the original trial, say the FDA skipped its usual independent advisory review, and they caution that claims of broad disease prevention go beyond what trials have shown.7 The professional guidelines kept their core advice: hormone therapy is for symptoms and bone protection, started before 60 or within 10 years of menopause, with the decision made one woman at a time.1,4

Age and time since menopause change the balance of benefits and risks

Under 60, or within 10 years

Benefits outweigh risks for most healthy women with symptoms

This is the group the guidelines most clearly support. Heart risk did not rise when women in their 50s started therapy.2,21

60 to 69, or 10 to 20 years out

Starting is a closer call that needs an individual decision

Absolute risks of heart disease, stroke, clots and dementia are higher when therapy starts later. Guidelines advise caution.2,21

70 and older

Starting hormone therapy at this age is not advised

Women who began in their 70s had more heart disease, and starting at 65 or older raised dementia risk in trials.21,2

These zones are about starting. Continuing is a separate question: there's no rule that you must stop at 65. Guidelines say to revisit the decision regularly, weighing your symptoms, bone health and personal risks.2,4

Patches and gels avoid the small rise in blood clot risk seen with estrogen pills

Estrogen taken by mouth passes through the liver first, which raises clotting factors. In a study of more than 80,000 women who had a blood clot, estrogen pills were linked to about nine extra clots per 10,000 women per year, while patches and gels showed no increase.33 Guidelines favor patches or gels for women with migraine with aura, diabetes, high blood pressure or a past clot, when hormone therapy is used at all.4

Body-identical hormones are available as regular FDA-approved prescriptions. Estradiol is the same estrogen the ovaries make, and micronized progesterone is the same progesterone. Micronized progesterone doesn't appear to raise clot risk and may carry less breast cancer risk than older synthetic progestins, although that evidence comes from observational studies.34,35 It's usually taken at bedtime because it can make you drowsy, and the common capsule contains peanut oil.2

Custom-compounded hormones, often marketed as bioidentical, are a different product. They aren't FDA-approved or tested for consistent dosing, and every major society recommends approved products instead. Saliva tests should not be used to set doses.2

The breast cancer risk from hormone therapy is small and depends on the type of therapy

With combined estrogen and progestogen, the added risk is rare: fewer than one extra breast cancer per 1,000 women per year of use, which is less than the risk linked to drinking two glasses of wine a day.35 In the large trial, women who took estrogen alone after a hysterectomy developed fewer breast cancers than women on placebo, and fewer died of breast cancer over long-term follow-up.35 Risk appears to rise with longer use, which is one reason the decision gets revisited over time.2

Hormone therapy is not recommended for preventing dementia or heart disease

European guidelines and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force both advise against taking hormone therapy only to prevent chronic disease.4,46 For the brain, starting at 65 or older raised dementia risk in trials, while women who used it for a few years right after menopause showed neither harm nor benefit to thinking skills ten years later.36 For the heart, starting in your 50s doesn't appear to raise risk, but it hasn't been proven to lower it either.21,4

Bone is the exception. Hormone therapy prevents bone loss and fractures, and European guidelines say it can be considered for bone protection in women under 60 even without hot flashes.4

Some women should not take hormone therapy that circulates through the body

Unexplained vaginal bleeding, breast cancer or another estrogen-sensitive cancer, active liver disease, or a possible pregnancy.2,1

A past heart attack, stroke or blood clot, or an inherited clotting disorder. Low-dose vaginal estrogen is often still possible, decided with your care team.2,5

During perimenopause, some birth control methods treat symptoms and prevent pregnancy at the same time

Hormone therapy uses lower doses than birth control and won't prevent pregnancy.43 If you still need contraception, a low-dose birth control pill can steady the hormonal swings and ease hot flashes, as long as you don't smoke and have no heart or clotting risks.39

A levonorgestrel IUD is another option. It lightens heavy periods and prevents pregnancy, and if you add estrogen for hot flashes, it also protects the uterine lining. ACOG describes this as an off-label use, with the IUD replaced after five years.39,1

Very heavy bleeding, bleeding between periods, and any bleeding after you've gone 12 months without a period should be checked by a clinician.

Vaginal and urinary symptoms respond well to low-dose vaginal estrogen, which is safe for most women

Low-dose vaginal estrogen comes as a cream, tablet, insert or ring. So little reaches the bloodstream that it doesn't need a progestogen, and routine checks of the uterine lining aren't required. The 2025 urology and urogynecology guideline found no evidence linking it to breast cancer and recommends it to reduce repeat urinary tract infections. For women with a history of breast cancer, it can be considered together with the cancer care team.5,2

Non-estrogen prescriptions include vaginal DHEA (prasterone) and ospemifene, a daily pill. Over-the-counter moisturizers and lubricants help with comfort, and pelvic floor physical therapy helps when muscles are part of the problem. Vaginal laser treatments aren't considered standard care.5

Testosterone can help low sexual desire after menopause, and that is its only evidence-based use

An international consensus of medical societies found that testosterone improves desire, arousal and satisfaction in postmenopausal women whose low desire causes them distress, when it's given through the skin at doses that keep blood levels in the normal range for younger women. Safety data don't go beyond about two years, and there's no FDA-approved testosterone product for women in the U.S., so it's prescribed off-label. The same consensus found the evidence doesn't support testosterone for any other symptom or condition, and it advises against compounded testosterone.11

A short, structured course of talk therapy that changes how you respond to hot flashes and sleep problems. It makes hot flashes less bothersome and helps sleep and mood.1,3,4

Clinical hypnosis

Guided sessions with a trained practitioner reduced hot flashes in trials and helped sleep.1,3,4

✕ Not supported

Supplements, herbs and gadgets

Black cohosh, soy isoflavones and other plant estrogens, and evening primrose oil have not been shown to work, and supplements aren't regulated for safety or purity. Paced breathing, cooling products and avoiding triggers haven't been shown to reduce hot flashes, although they're harmless to try for comfort. Cannabis products haven't been shown to help either.1,3,4

Habits and screenings started during the transition protect bone, muscle, heart and brain later in life

Women can now spend up to 40% of their lives after menopause.17 Because several of the biggest changes happen in a short window, the transition is a good time to put the most useful habits in place.

Heavy strength training with impact builds bone, and calcium and vitamin D work best from food

Lift heavy, gradually, with supervision at first

In an Australian trial, women in their 60s with low bone density did supervised heavy lifting and jumping for 30 minutes twice a week. After eight months they had gained bone density in the spine and hip and stood slightly taller, with no fractures from the training.37 If you already have osteoporosis, start with a trainer or physical therapist.

Get calcium and vitamin D mainly from what you eat

Daily targets are about 1,000 to 1,200 mg of calcium and 600 to 800 IU of vitamin D, from all sources. Low-dose supplements haven't been shown to prevent fractures in women without a deficiency, and calcium plus vitamin D supplements slightly raise kidney stone risk.9

Know when to get a bone density scan

All women should have one at 65. If you're past menopause and younger than 65, ask about a risk check if you have a risk factor such as a parent who broke a hip, low body weight, smoking, heavy drinking or long-term steroid use.8

Strength training at least twice a week, with enough protein, protects muscle

U.S. activity guidelines call for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days.19 Only about 7% of women going through the transition meet the activity guidelines.17

For protein, an international expert group recommends 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight a day for adults over 65, and more for those who exercise. For a 150-pound woman, that works out to roughly 68 to 82 grams a day.18 Research in midlife women is thinner, and one short trial in postmenopausal women who were lifting weights found no extra muscle gain from eating more protein than the standard recommendation.40 Getting enough protein is sensible; very high intakes aren't proven to add much.

Checking heart risk factors in your 40s and 50s catches the changes the transition brings

The American Heart Association calls the menopause transition a key window for early prevention. Ask for your blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and blood sugar to be checked, and keep an eye on your waist size, since belly fat carries extra risk even at a normal weight.17 Limited trial data suggest that combined lifestyle programs during the transition can prevent weight gain and improve blood pressure, triglycerides and blood sugar.17 Not smoking matters too; smoking also brings on menopause about one to two years earlier.44

Treating insomnia and depression protects the brain more reliably than any supplement

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia in adults. Sleeping pills are meant for short stretches, while the skills learned in CBT-I keep working over the long term, with fewer harms. It's offered in person, by phone, online and through self-help books.10 Chronic insomnia has also been linked to faster biological aging.32

For long-term brain health, researchers point to steps with broad evidence, such as keeping blood pressure in range and staying active. Hormone therapy should not be started for the purpose of preventing dementia.4,45

Daily sunscreen is the best-proven way to slow skin aging

In a randomized trial of adults under 55, people who applied broad-spectrum sunscreen every morning showed no detectable increase in skin aging over four and a half years, and 24% less aging than people who used it only some of the time.38 Estrogen therapy increases skin collagen and thickness in studies, but it isn't approved or recommended for skin alone.31

Some popular menopause treatments lack evidence or carry avoidable risks

  • Compounded hormones and hormone pellets, which aren't FDA-approved.2
  • Saliva tests or hormone panels used to set doses.2,4
  • Estrogen doses above approved ranges, and testosterone for anything other than low sexual desire. The British Menopause Society reported growing use of both in 2026.15
  • Supplements marketed for menopause.1
  • Starting hormone therapy at 65 or older to prevent disease.4,36

Researchers are still working out several open questions

  • How long it's safe to continue hormone therapy past 60 or 65, since few trials have tested it.2
  • Whether starting estrogen early protects the brain; trials so far are neutral.36
  • Whether micronized progesterone carries less breast cancer risk than other progestogens.35
  • Long-term safety of testosterone and of the newer neurokinin blockers.11,13
  • How weight-loss medicines such as GLP-1 drugs work alongside hormone therapy.15
  • How best to treat perimenopause itself, which has been studied far less than the years after menopause.4,23
